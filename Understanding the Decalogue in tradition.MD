In Jewish liturgical and Masoretic tradition, those two systems are known as **Ta'am Elyon** (טַעַם עֶלְיוֹן — the "Upper Accent") and **Ta'am Tachton** (טַעַם תַּחְתּוֹן — the "Lower Accent"). 

In traditional synagogue practice, **a reader never sings both at the same time**. It is strictly an *either/or* performance:

1. **Ta'am Elyon (Public / Festive Reading):**  
   Chanted publicly on **Shavuot** (the Festival of Weeks, celebrating the Giving of the Torah) and, in many Ashkenazi and Sephardi communities, during the annual Sabbath readings of Parashat *Yitro* and *Va'etchanan*.  
   * **The Logic:** The text is partitioned strictly by the **Ten Commandments themselves**. A long commandment (like the prohibition of idols, Exod 20:3–6) is chanted as one massive, uninterrupted dramatic utterance; conversely, the four short, staccato prohibitions—*“Do not murder. Do not commit adultery. Do not steal. Do not bear false witness”*—each stand alone as complete, independent sentences ending on major pauses (*silluq* and *atnah*).
2. **Ta'am Tachton (Private Study & Ordinary Reading):**  
   Designed for private study (*keriat ha-torah be-yachid*) and ordinary reading.  
   * **The Logic:** The text is re-punctuated into **standard biblical verse lengths** (the standard 12 verses in Exodus and 13 in Deuteronomy). Because ancient Jewish law generally discouraged verses shorter than three words, *Ta'am Tachton* bundles the short commandments together into a single, grammatically balanced verse (Exod 20:13/14) with conventional sub-pauses (*zaqef-qatan*, *revia*, *atnah*).

### How They Are Divided in Print

Because a Torah reader (*Ba'al Koreh*) cannot read a composite score on the fly, publishers of liturgical workbooks and scholarly editions routinely divide them:

* **In a *Tikkun Kor'im* (Torah Reader's Manual):**  
  Almost every standard *Tikkun* (such as the classic *Rödelheim*, *Tikkun Simanim*, or *ArtScroll*) prints the Decalogue **twice**. Typically, you turn the page and see the Decalogue set out entirely in *Ta'am Tachton* (for private preparation or regular Shabbat), followed immediately by the complete passage printed again with only *Ta'am Elyon* (for Shavuot).
* **In Jewish Bibles (*Chumashim*):**  
  In editions like the *Koren Tanakh* or the *Hertz Chumash*, the main running text usually carries the double accents compiled together (or the lower accents with upper superscripts), while a dedicated appendix at the back prints both readings in **parallel facing columns** so the reader can trace each melodic and syntactic journey separately.
* **In Masoretic Scholarship:**  
  Christian David Ginsburg, in his 1897 *Introduction to the Massoretico-Critical Edition of the Hebrew Bible*, famously printed the Exodus and Deuteronomy Decalogues in twin columns, demonstrating how the Masoretes layered an ideological/liturgical division (commandment-by-commandment) directly over a syntactic verse division.

### The Genius of Haïk-Vantoura’s Synthesis

What makes Suzanne Haïk-Vantoura’s deciphering key so arresting by comparison is that **she refused to treat the parchment as a typographical compromise**. 

While historical-critical grammarians assume the Masoretes were merely saving parchment by superimposing two incompatible liturgical tracks onto a single text, Haïk-Vantoura approached the marks as an **integrated, intentional musical composition**. 

When both accents are sung together:
* You don't get a grammatical clash; you get **dense, expressive melismas**.
* Those stacked supralinear ornaments and compound sublinear accents produce dramatic intervals. As noted in your *Genesis* commentary regarding the rare *atnah* + *silluq* combination (bar 225 on Genesis 35:22 and throughout the Decalogue in Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 5), the music drops a descending fourth on a single syllable (\\(A \to e\\), subdominant to tonic). It functions as profound word-painting—a cadence of rest momentarily announced and then starkly resolved.

In synagogue tradition, dividing them into public and private tracks tames the text into standard cantillation formulas. But in the Haïk-Vantoura realization, keeping them united turns the Decalogue into a monumental, aurally arresting vocal tour de force.
